The R community in Geneva, for everyone +

RLadies+ Geneva is the Geneva chapter of the global RLadies+ network. Our goal is to bring more women and underrepresented people into the R community — through free, welcoming meetups, talks and hands-on workshops. We're a diverse, open group, and everyone who supports that goal is welcome to join.

about

A local chapter of a worldwide movement

RLadies+ is a worldwide organisation whose mission is to promote gender diversity in the R community. R is one of the most widely used languages for statistics, data science and reproducible research — and the people who use it should reflect the world it describes.

In Geneva, we bring that mission home: a friendly space for R users across the region's universities, hospitals, international organisations, NGOs and industry to learn from each other, present their work, and grow — whatever their level.

No prior experience is required. Curiosity is enough.

You don't have to be a woman or from an underrepresented group to belong. Our meetups are open to all genders — men included. What unites us is the shared goal, not who we are.

evidence

Why a community like this matters

The gap in data science and statistics is not a story about ability — it is about who feels they belong, and that is something a community can change. Here is what the evidence shows.

the gap is real

~11%

About 11% of authors of R packages are women — the people who build the tools the whole community depends on. That share has barely moved from roughly 9% in 2010.

Forwards · R Foundation task force, 2017
< 1 in 5

Fewer than one in five data scientists are women. On Kaggle, the field's largest community, women have hovered between about 16% and 22% of participants for years.

Kaggle surveys · peer-reviewed analysis, 2023
34% → 16%

Women are about a third of scientific authors worldwide, but only ~16% of the most-cited researchers. The gap widens with seniority — exactly where the role models thin out.

Quantitative Science Studies, 2022

what actually moves the numbers

100% stayed

Role models keep women in

In a randomised field experiment, first-year women in engineering given a female peer mentor had 100% retention in their major after one year — versus 82% with a male mentor and 89% with none. Belonging and confidence, not GPA, best predicted who stayed.

Dennehy & Dasgupta, 2017 · PNAS
effect persisted

And the effect lasts

Followed to graduation and a year beyond, the same women showed higher STEM-degree completion, more internships, and sustained ambition to continue. A brief early intervention, durable results.

Wu, Thiem & Dasgupta, 2022 · Nature Communications
more voice

Peers change participation

Women placed alongside female peers in small technical groups spoke up more, felt more motivated, and reported stronger career aspirations than those doing identical work in male-dominated groups.

Dasgupta, Scircle & Hunsinger, 2015 · PNAS

These trials isolate the exact mechanisms a chapter like ours is built to supply: visible role models, a peer network, and a sense of belonging. RLadies+ Geneva has not been run as a controlled trial — but the ingredients the evidence points to are precisely the ones we put in one room, every meetup. And the stakes are not only fairness: the European Commission estimates that closing the digital gender gap could add roughly €260–600 billion to EU GDP.
